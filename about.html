The Story of IntentSpider and Why Spider?

The fluid language web for textual tension and prediction.

Written by Neksha DeSilva on 30th of July, 2026.


The spark of IntentSpider began about 6 months ago, in January 2026. At that time I was living in an unstable situation, in a small room near a garage. My days were spent preparing for competitive programming olympiads, learning C++, and solving problems, while working on other things at the same time. One evening I was reading the essay "My Techno-Optimism" by Vitalik Buterin, which has accidently arrived to my mail inbox by a newsletyter that I didnt even subscribed for, called 80,000 Hours Newsletter. and that led me to reading about Bitcoin. I grew up with a strong bias against cryptocurrency. From a very early age, the words attached to it in my mind were scammer, blackmail, and dark web. I had never actually studied it, so I decided to look into it properly instead of just carrying that image around without knowing why. I researched how cryptography works, how blockchains work, and how cryptocurrency exchanges operate. This research did not correct that image, it reinforced it. I recalled an event from November 2022, when a major cryptocurrency exchange collapsed under fraud allegations, with reports of around 8 billion dollars in missing customer funds. I wanted to understand why so many people, including people with very little background in advanced mathematics or abstract systems, placed this much trust in the people running these exchanges. Then I realized that it was not about the kind of trust that most VCs, investors, stock buyers and traders, or even gamblers put into something that they believe. It was about the illusion of control of the data, and about people putting value on a future expectation instead of the present reality. Remember what I said, it was "illusion of colntrol over data". But, as obvious, that was an illusion. Watching real money fluctuate by the millisecond on these exchanges is the part that stuck with me, not the cryptography. That fluctuation is what actually pulled my interest away from cryptocurrency and toward information theory instead. To be honest, I was not interested in the fundemental layer of cryptography, that the cryprtography relies on a estimation that the power required to trace and reverse every cryptographic transaction on that specific blockchain at that given moment is a magnitude that current computers are unable to posses. Maybe. But maybe not. But rather, I was interested in how large amounts of data could be manipulated, patterned, and generated by paying close attention to a seemingly small data packets to carry meaning at scale, This was also similar to one of my billions of reasons I love computers. I thought the same process that a one software copy runs identically across billions of separate machines at once. I am also someone who pays a lot of attention to brains. And cognition and the conciousness in general. While I was working on a USACO problem one night, at about 7 or 8 PM, I looked over at the right corner of the room and saw a spider. I assumed at first that its web was one flat plane, the way people usually picture a web. It was not that. It was several webs, layered and crossing each other, more denser in some areas where they crossed. A cricket flew into it and got caught and it was struggling. A fairly large domestic cricket, larger than what I would expect a spider with that body size to prey. The spider was not unusual. So please do not assume it as a Goliath bird eating spider, or a large American black widow. It was an ordinary room spider, This is a very common type of spiders. It was resting at the far corner of the large web structure. It did not search for the cricket. It went straight to it, at a speed I did not expect at all, without appearing to navigate around any of the layers crossing each other in between. That is the part that lead me to stop touch typing (53 WPM), stop the work and think. If a normal animal had to cover that same distance across a structure like that, it would need to take several turns, because the nets were overlapping and intersecting in multiple places. This spider did not take any turns. It moved almost like it was floating. I actually considered for a moment whether this could be one of the spider species that can float short distances using air, since their body is light enough for that. But this was not one of those species, so that was an incorrect explaination. and it took me a while to avoid that thought from distracting me to understand the behaviour more about the spider. I looked into it afterward, and as also explained in a research paper, I learned that orb weaver spiders are one of the animals that are having the least amount of pheripheral vision. they are nearly blind and they do not capture or hunt for prey with their vision. They rely entirely on vibration and touch carried through their web. Yes, now I know why Spider web building is a primitive reflex of their brain rather than choice. That also explains the direct path. But it does not explain every question. Orb weaver spiders do not have a frontal lobe, or neurological structure that is necessary for executive function or complex planning. The Spider's primitive brain is their Ceribral Ganglion. The path this spider took, and the entire layered structure it built, is executed through pre-programmed evolutionary reflex that are evolved for about 200 millions years (Jurrasic age) as of today, not from conscious throughts. That is very unusual. I was thinking about this even more. If the spider cannot think through any of this, then the prediction must arrive from a design that physical sciences repeated and refined over a very long time, i was deeply aware of the perfectionism of the nature. Once the Coronavirus 19 came to the world, I later realized after a lecture about DNA mutation that, the mutated, but somehow survived DNA can be tolerent to faliure. Both in terms of its physical properties agaisnt the threat and efficiency. Then I later realized that it was one of the good reasons that are worth understanding rather than dismissing as normal animal bahaviour. These kinds of designs are likely to mirror the same basic principles that show up everywhere else in physics and in nature generally, and efficiency, as mentioned, one of those principles that I kept in my mind as a valuable output. Beacuse, nature always default physical matter to the least energy consumption. In chemistry, this is called, "stable state". And it is true. At that point I stopped what I was doing on the computer and went to take a closer look at the spider web. That is when I realized that there were a number of small rolling isopods trapped across it, the ones commonly called pill bugs. Some of them were still alive and still struggling to escape. Then I made another realization that, the weight of each one of those on the web, would change the physical dynamics of a vibration signal traveled through the rest of the structure. A signal reaching the spider is not shaped only by the struggling prey. But rather, it is also calculated by all other objects already on that same structure at the same moment of time. That detail became the basis for the residue arbitration process described later in the paper. Credit for that part of the system genuinely belongs to those isopods.

I kept researching after that and came across Ant Colony Optimization, a framework built using the dynamics of pheromone trails, a substance ants release that reinforces a path when it gets used often and decays when it does not. That gave me a second piece to work with, alongside the vibration and the layered web structure, for representing connections between words as something that gets stronger and weaker over time instead of staying fixed once it is set. The first prototypes did not work. For months this felt closer to science fiction than to something that could actually run inside a keyboard. What eventually made it work was changing how I thought about the whole problem. Instead of treating typed text as a flat sequence to store and match against, I started treating it as a set of connections that form a shape, the same way that web was never one flat surface but something layered and load bearing in specific places. Compared to this, the keyboards already on the market, Gboard, SwiftKey, QuickType, and the rest, are relatively simple. They match recent word patterns against a statistical model, store what a person types, and in some cases send that data to private cloud servers. That is a privacy problem on its own. But privacy was not where I started. I started with accuracy and efficiency. Privacy came out of building something that runs entirely on one device, it was not the original goal. The existing approach also behaves more like a flash card system than anything alive. It stores, and it matches. It does not reshape itself as a person's habits actually change over time. I also noticed something happening across the industry more broadly. Device manufacturers keep reducing on device storage while increasing memory and processing power, and most of that added capacity is going toward running larger AI models. Under that trend, putting a full AI language model directly inside a keyboard becomes less and less worth it for a company, unless that company has no interest in profit at all. That gap, between what a keyboard could actually be and what companies have any real incentive to build, is where IntentSpider sits. The full paper goes through these analogies in detail, along with the actual mathematics behind each one. My goal now is to publish this in research venues, make it open source, and get it into the hands of researchers who can take it further than I have on my own.
