About IntentSpider


IntentSpider began about five months ago, in February 2026. At that time I was living in an unstable situation, in a small room near a garage. My days were spent preparing for competitive programming olympiads, learning C++, and solving problems, while working on other things at the same time. One evening I was reading about the history of technology, and that led me to reading about Bitcoin. I grew up with a strong bias against cryptocurrency. From a very early age, the words attached to it in my mind were scammer, blackmail, and dark web. I had never actually studied it, so I decided to look into it properly instead of just carrying that image around without knowing why. I researched how cryptography works, how blockchains work, and how cryptocurrency exchanges operate. This research did not correct that image, it reinforced it. I recalled an event from November 2022, when a major cryptocurrency exchange collapsed under fraud allegations, with reports of around eight billion dollars in missing customer funds. I wanted to understand why so many people, including people with very little background in advanced mathematics or abstract systems, placed this much trust in the people running these exchanges. What I landed on was that it was not really about trust. It was about the illusion of control, and about people putting value on a future expectation instead of the present reality. Watching real money fluctuate by the millisecond on these exchanges is the part that stuck with me, not the cryptography. That fluctuation is what actually pulled my interest away from cryptocurrency and toward information theory instead. I was not interested in securing data. I was interested in how large amounts of data could be manipulated, patterned, and made to carry meaning at scale, the same way one piece of software runs identically across millions of separate machines at once. I am also someone who pays a lot of attention to nature, and this next part is where that comes in. While I was working on a problem one night, I looked over at the corner of the room and saw a spider. I assumed at first that its web was one flat plane, the way people usually picture a web. It was not that. It was several webs, layered and crossing each other, denser in the places where they crossed. A cricket flew into it and got caught, a fairly large domestic cricket, larger than what I would expect a spider that size to take down easily. The spider itself was nothing unusual. It was not a Goliath bird eating spider, and it was not as large as an American black widow either. It was an ordinary house spider, the kind anyone would walk past without noticing. It was resting at the far corner of the whole structure. It did not search for the cricket. It went straight to it, at a speed I did not expect at all, without appearing to navigate around any of the layers crossing each other in between. That part is what stopped me. If a normal animal had to cover that same distance across a structure like that, it would need to take several turns, because the nets were overlapping and intersecting in multiple places. This spider did not take any turns. It moved almost like it was floating. I actually considered for a moment whether this could be one of the spider species that can float short distances using air, since their body is light enough for that. But this was not one of those species, so that explanation did not hold up, and it took me a while to let go of it. I looked into it afterward and learned that orb weaver spiders are not just weak sighted, they are functionally blind. They do not locate prey by sight at all. They rely entirely on vibration and touch carried through the web. That explains the direct path. It does not explain everything, though. Orb weaver spiders do not have a frontal lobe, or anything close to the brain structure needed for executive function or complex planning. The path this spider took, and the entire layered structure it built, comes from hard coded evolutionary reflex, not from anything the spider consciously worked out. That is what made me stop and actually think about it for a while. If the spider cannot think through any of this, then the precision has to come from somewhere else entirely, from a design nature repeated and refined over a very long time, for reasons that are worth understanding rather than dismissing as just a normal thing animals do. These kinds of designs tend to mirror the same basic principles that show up everywhere else in physics and in nature generally, and efficiency is one of those principles that shows up constantly. Nature tends to default to whatever costs the least and works the most reliably. At that point I stopped what I was doing on the computer and went to take a closer look at the web itself. That is when I noticed there were a number of small isopods scattered across it, the ones commonly called pill bugs. Some of them were still alive and still struggling to get free. It occurred to me that the weight of each one of those, sitting on the web, would change how a vibration signal traveled through the rest of the structure. A signal reaching the spider is not shaped only by whatever triggered it. It is shaped by everything else already sitting on that same structure at the same time. That detail became the basis for the residue arbitration process described later in the paper. Credit for that part of the system genuinely belongs to those isopods. I kept researching after that and came across Ant Colony Optimization, a framework built around pheromone trails, a substance ants release that reinforces a path when it gets used often and decays when it does not. That gave me a second piece to work with, alongside the vibration and the layered web structure, for representing connections between words as something that gets stronger and weaker over time instead of staying fixed once it is set. The first prototypes did not work. For months this felt closer to science fiction than to something that could actually run inside a keyboard. What eventually made it work was changing how I thought about the whole problem. Instead of treating typed text as a flat sequence to store and match against, I started treating it as a set of connections that form a shape, the same way that web was never one flat surface but something layered and load bearing in specific places. Compared to this, the keyboards already on the market, Gboard, SwiftKey, QuickType, and the rest, are relatively simple. They match recent word patterns against a statistical model, store what a person types, and in some cases send that data to private cloud servers. That is a privacy problem on its own. But privacy was not where I started. I started with accuracy and efficiency. Privacy came out of building something that runs entirely on one device, it was not the original goal. The existing approach also behaves more like a flash card system than anything alive. It stores, and it matches. It does not reshape itself as a person's habits actually change over time. I also noticed something happening across the industry more broadly. Device manufacturers keep reducing on device storage while increasing memory and processing power, and most of that added capacity is going toward running larger AI models. Under that trend, putting a full AI language model directly inside a keyboard becomes less and less worth it for a company, unless that company has no interest in profit at all. That gap, between what a keyboard could actually be and what companies have any real incentive to build, is where IntentSpider sits. The full paper goes through these analogies in detail, along with the actual mathematics behind each one. Thanks are also owed to the NASA astronauts of Apollo 11, and to Professor Claude Shannon, the founder of information theory. My goal now is to publish this in research venues, make it open source, and get it into the hands of researchers who can take it further than I have on my own. IntentSpider is The Fluid Language Web for Textual Tension and Prediction. Thanks Everyone.
